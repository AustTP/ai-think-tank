# Pricing

## Purpose
Reach for this before setting, revising, or presenting a pricing structure for
a product the think tank ships or a client project. Produces a value-based tier
structure with the assumptions behind it -- not a three-column guess.

## Key facts
- Price by value delivered, not cost-plus or "what feels fair." Anchor to the
  customer's perceived value of the outcome the product enables.
- The 4C pricing model: Costs (floor), Competitors (market anchor), Customers
  (willingness to pay / value), Constraints (ceilings from the other three).
- Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter: ask customers 4 questions (too
  expensive, too cheap, expensive but would still buy, cheap but seems like a
  bargain) to find the acceptable price range and the indifference point. Use
  survey results, not gut feel.
- 3-tier "good / better / best" structure is the norm: the middle tier is the
  anchor buyers choose; the top tier's job is to make the middle look
  reasonable. Price per user / seat or per outcome, not per "premium feature
  salad."
- Avoid the race-to-the-bottom: value-based pricing with clear tiers beats
  "cheaper than the cheapest competitor" on both revenue and customer quality.
- Never invent price-test data. If willingness-to-pay is unknown, name it as
  the open decision and propose the cheapest valid way to learn it (surveys,
  pre-orders, launch tiers) before locking a number.

## Policy for this think tank
1. Every price must carry the reasoning: floor, anchor, and the value
  assumption it encodes. A number with no reasoning is a guess.
2. Tiers must differ by value, not just price; label the target buyer of each
  tier.
3. Flag any number based on assumption (not data) as `[assumption]` so the
  team knows what to validate first.
4. Offer to run or design a Van Westendorp survey rather than fabricating
  responses.

## Lessons learned
- The most common failure is a pricing page with no reasoning behind it. Lead
  with the value anchor, and the structure stops looking arbitrary.
